Intellectual property and copyright
Before issuing a solicitation or awarding a contract, you must identify any intellectual property (IP) that exists before contract award and any IP that will be generated as a result of the contract. This will help reduce future costs and avoid disputes.
IP is anything resulting from a contract that can be copyrighted, trademarked, patented, licensed, etc. Although any contract could have IP , the likelihood is much greater if the contract will aim for something new or might incorporate new processes. IP considerations are most relevant to contracts for research and development (R&D), for software development or where new written material will be produced.
Refer to the following policy and guide for the complete details on IP in procurement:
Policy on Title to Intellectual Property Arising Under Crown Procurement Contract
Implementation Guide: Policy on the Title to Intellectual Property Arising Under Crown Procurement Contracts
Canada’s default position is to allow contractors to retain the rights to IP generated under government contracts, to promote the commercialization of new ideas given the private sector’s greater capacity for commercialization. Allowable exceptions to the default position are described in the policy and guide referenced above.
At the planning stage, you must determine what IP is anticipated, how IP will be handled and how it will be identified and secured for the use of the client and Canada. This process can have very long timeframes and extremely complex processes. Identifying the foreground and background information early is crucial, as delays can disadvantage Canada severely.
The client must decide the extent to which IP rights are to be retained by Canada. You should discuss with the client their needs to ensure they are aware of the extent to which you can obtain the rights they need to use the IP created under the contract, whether Canada or the contractor owns the IP . Depending on industry practice, Canada may not be able to obtain IP ownership even if desired. In such cases, PSPC contract clauses allow clients to use contractor-owned IP , although Canada could not commercialize the IP .
The client should be requested to fully justify any request to retain ownership of IP , except for the case of non-software copyright, where for PSPC buyers the norm is for Canada to retain ownership.
