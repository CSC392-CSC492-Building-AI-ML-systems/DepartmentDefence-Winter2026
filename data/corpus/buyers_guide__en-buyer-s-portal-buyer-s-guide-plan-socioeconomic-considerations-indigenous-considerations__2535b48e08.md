Indigenous considerations Indigenous considerations in procurement aim to support the participation of Indigenous businesses while ensuring compliance with obligations under Comprehensive Land Claims Agreements (CLCAs) and the Procurement Strategy for Indigenous Business (PSIB). While both CLCA s and PSIB promote Indigenous involvement, they are distinct frameworks with specific guidelines. Understanding how these work together is crucial, as certain procurements may be subject to both. Indigenous considerations: A unified approach Indigenous considerations in procurement are governed by both the CLCA s and the PSIB . CLCA s and PSIB are distinct and should not be confused. Key distinctions and integrations In certain cases, a procurement subject to CLCA contracting obligations may also be set-aside under PSIB . The procedures for both can be applied, provided that PSIB set-asides do not interfere with the contracting obligations under a CLCA . When conflicts arise between the two, CLCA obligations take precedence. If a CLCA grants a right of first refusal to Indigenous businesses, the procurement cannot be set-aside under PSIB . The PSIB clauses cannot be used or modified to implement the CLCA right of first refusal. If a CLCA does not include a right of first refusal, the procurement can be reserved to Indigenous businesses across Canada under PSIB while still addressing the CLCA contracting obligations, including any CLCA evaluation criteria. The act of setting aside a procurement under PSIB does not, by itself, address the CLCA procurement obligations. The various procurement obligations of the applicable CLCA will still have to be considered. A solicitation of offers subject to a PSIB set-aside that addresses CLCA evaluation criteria must clearly define what qualifies as a CLCA beneficiary. This clarification helps prevent confusion with the definition of "Indigenous business" under PSIB . Support and sourcing For help identifying potential Indigenous suppliers under PSIB set-asides, see Procurement Strategy for Indigenous Business . You must make every reasonable effort to meet operational needs while achieving best value, and observing the principles of prudence, probity and sound contracting management. Any discrepancies in guidance must be reported to IPPD and all received advice must be thoroughly documented in the procurement file. Decision to set aside When determining whether to set aside a procurement under PSIB , the decision rests with the client. There are two types of PSIB set-asides, as follows: Mandatory set-asides A procurement must be set aside under PSIB if the primary recipients or end user of the goods or services are an Indigenous population and the procurement value exceeds $5000. Provided that operational needs, prudence, probity, best value and sound contract management can be assured. For an Indigenous population to be the primary recipient or end user of the goods or services, delivery does not have to be directly to the Indigenous community. For instance, goods may be delivered to a government site before distribution to Indigenous communities, groups or individuals. Refer to Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat (TBS) Contracting Policy Notice 1996-2 for information on the meaning of Indigenous population. For examples and more information on mandatory set-asides under PSIB , please contact Indigenous Services Canada . Voluntary set-asides Clients may choose to restrict any procurement exclusively to qualified Indigenous businesses. You should assist clients in meeting their objectives by highlighting potential opportunities for voluntary PSIB set-asides when qualified Indigenous businesses are known to exist in the marketplace. If no Indigenous business submits a responsive offer, or if no contract is awarded despite receiving offers to avoid conflicting with sound contracting principles, the solicitation of offers must be reissued. It can be done as either a set-aside or open to all offerors, depending on trade agreement procedures and applicable thresholds. PSPC will not unilaterally declare a procurement set-aside under PSIB . If a requisition exceeding $5000 identifies an Indigenous population as the primary recipient or end user and is not designated as a PSIB set-aside, you should consult with the client. If the client decides against a set-aside, this decision should be recorded in the file, and the procurement can proceed. Indigenous business certification Offerors must certify that they are an Indigenous business as defined under the PSIB . This certification must assert the offeror's ongoing compliance with the criteria which define it as an Indigenous business throughout the contract's duration. An offeror’s certification that it is Indigenous is subject to audit, before and after a contract is awarded, to ensure compliance. Subcontracting to Indigenous businesses Clients may specify that a proportion of subcontracts in open procurements be reserved for Indigenous businesses. This can also include encouraging non-Indigenous offerors to subcontract to Indigenous businesses, through the use of incentives such as additional evaluation points. The inclusion of Indigenous businesses as subcontractors must be clearly identified in the solicitation of offers as an evaluation criterion. Such practices are permitted by all of Canada’s free trade agreements. Set-asides and Canadian content If the value of a procurement is $25,000 or more, both the PSIB and the Canadian Content Policy will apply simultaneously. Here’s how these guidelines interact within the framework of set-aside procurements: Certification levels There are two levels of certification under a set-aside procurement. A business must certify its status as an Indigenous business to qualify for consideration. This foundational step ensures only Indigenous businesses are eligible for consideration in the set-aside procurement process. Applying the Canadian Content Policy After establishing that the procurement qualifies as a PSIB set-aside, the Canadian Content Policy is applied in the same manner as any other procurement. You must determine whether there are sufficient eligible Indigenous businesses to carry out the procurement under one of three categories: Solely limited: two or more Indigenous businesses can supply the required Canadian goods or services Conditionally limited: potential exists for two or more Indigenous businesses offering Canadian goods or services Open: there is an insufficient number of Indigenous businesses able to provide Canadian goods or services; thus, the procurement is open to all qualified Indigenous businesses, regardless of the goods or services’ origin Review process An offer that includes Canadian content provisions under a set-aside procurement must first be reviewed to ensure the business has provided the necessary certification that it is an Indigenous business. Those offers that meet this criterion are then further evaluated against the Canadian content criteria. Adherence to sound contracting principles Fundamental to all PSIB procurements is the need to adhere to sound contracting principles. You must always be cognizant of the principles of best value, prudence, probity and operational requirements, in planning your procurement strategy for PSIB set-aside requirements. Red River Métis Business Directory The Government of Canada recognizes the Manitoba Métis Federation's Red River Métis Business Directory. Businesses listed in the Red River Métis Business Directory are eligible for federal procurement opportunities, such as under the Procurement Strategy for Indigenous Business (PSIB). Comprehensive Land Claims Agreements If you or your clients are aware that a procurement will occur in a location subject to an ongoing Land Claim, or an area for which a Comprehensive Land Claims Agreement (CLCA) or Modern Treaty is being negotiated, you or your client should contact the Indigenous Procurement Policy Case Management Team . Applicability of CLCA s contracting obligations When procuring goods, services or construction that will be delivered to locations covered by CLCA s, the contracting obligations of each applicable CLCA s apply to each associated portion of the procurement. The final delivery points, which are not necessarily the destination addresses detailed in the requisition, determine the applicability of a CLCA s, not the origin of the requisition. Additional scenarios where CLCA s procurement obligations may apply include the following: procurements in support of government activities within a CLCA s area procurements involving performance of services or associated travel by the resulting contractor within a CLCA s area You should consult with the appropriate advisory body to determine if CLCA s apply in these situations. The key procedural elements are as follows: dollar thresholds: CLCA s apply to applicable procurements of any value overlapping areas: in cases where settlement areas of different CLCA s overlap, the obligations of both CLCA s apply. For example, Inuvik, Northwest Territories is situated within the settlement areas of both the Inuvialuit Final Agreement and the Gwich'in CLCA , and so the contracting obligations of both CLCA s will apply to the portion of the procurement with deliveries to Inuvik urgent requirements: procurements for urgent requirements must be handled on a case-by-case basis in accordance with the applicable CLCA For procurements that are for pressing emergencies as defined by Treasury Board Secretariat Contracting Policy Notice 2007-4 on Non-Competitive Contracting , you should seek assistance. Impact of trade agreements on Indigenous procurements Canada's free trade agreements pose no impediment to the inclusion of measures for the benefit of Indigenous Peoples and/or businesses in a procurement. When a procurement is set-aside for Indigenous businesses under PSIB , it is no longer subject to the obligations of the trade agreements and does not fall under the jurisdiction of the Canadian International Trade Tribunal. Canadian Free Trade Agreement Under Article 800: Aboriginal Peoples of the Canadian Free Trade Agreement (CFTA), the CFTA does not apply to any measure adopted or maintained with respect to Indigenous Peoples. If a measure is adopted or maintained with respect to Indigenous Peoples and or businesses in a CFTA -covered procurement, some or all aspects of the procurement may not be subject to CFTA obligations. To reduce risk, you should continue to follow the CFTA obligations to the greatest extent possible. Deviations should be limited and only occur when necessary to implement the measure(s) for Indigenous peoples/businesses. ### Indigenous considerations: A unified approach

Indigenous considerations in procurement are governed by both the CLCA s and the PSIB . CLCA s and PSIB are distinct and should not be confused.

#### Key distinctions and integrations

In certain cases, a procurement subject to CLCA contracting obligations may also be set-aside under PSIB . When conflicts arise between the two, CLCA obligations take precedence.

### Procurement Strategy for Indigenous Business

All federal government departments are encouraged to set-aside a portion of procurements for competition by Indigenous businesses, provided operational needs are fully met. This approach aims to foster participation and competitiveness among Indigenous businesses.

Despite a procurement being designated under the PSIB , all applicable procurement policies and procedures must be adhered to. PSPC buyers seeking policy guidance on PSIB can consult with the Indigenous Procurement Policy Case Management Team .

Any discrepancies in guidance must be reported to IPPD and all received advice must be thoroughly documented in the procurement file. #### Set-aside program requirements for Indigenous business

Indigenous businesses eligible for the set-aside program must be at least 51% Indigenous owned and controlled and can be :

- a band as defined by the Indian Act
- sole proprietorships
- limited companies
- cooperatives
- partnerships
- not-for-profit organizations

Joint ventures consisting of two or more Indigenous businesses or an Indigenous business and non-Indigenous businesses are eligible if the Indigenous business has at least 51% ownership and control of the joint venture.

##### Certification requirements

The offeror must certify in its submitted offer that it is an Indigenous business or a joint venture constituted and that they meet the above ownership and control criteria as described above. As part of its offer, the offeror must complete the certification of requirements for the set-aside program for Indigenous business (certification) stating that it:

- meets the requirements for the program and will continue to do so throughout the duration of the contract
- will, upon request, provide evidence that it meets the eligibility criteria
- is willing to be audited regarding the certification
- acknowledges that if it is found not to meet the eligibility criteria, the offeror shall be subject to one or more of the civil consequences set out in the certification and the contract

##### Additional requirements

If an offer involves subcontracting, the offeror must certify in its offer that at least thirty-three percent of the value of the work will be performed by an Indigenous business.

The offeror must notify the subcontractor and where applicable bind the subcontractor in writing with respect to the requirements that the Indigenous set-aside program may impose on the subcontractor.

The offeror’s contract with a subcontractor must also, where applicable, include a provision in which the subcontractor agrees to provide the offeror with information, substantiating that it meets the Indigenous set-aside program requirements and also authorize the offeror to have an audit performed by Canada to verify their compliance. Failure by the offeror to exact or enforce such provision will be deemed to be a breach of contract and subject to several civil consequences, including contract termination and financial penalties.

##### Proof of eligibility

Evidence of eligibility is not required at the offer stage, but must be available for audits.

The civil consequences for false statements in the offer documents, non-compliance with the requirements of the set-aside program or failing to produce satisfactory evidence to Canada regarding the requirements may include:

- the forfeiture of the offer deposit
- retention of holdbacks
- disqualification from future contracts
- potential termination of the contract with liabilities for any additional costs incurred by Canada

##### Required evidence from businesses

Documented evidence of ownership and control of an Indigenous business or joint venture may include:

- incorporation documents
- shareholders’ or members’ register
- partnership agreements
- joint venture agreements
- business name registration
- banking arrangements
- governance records of meeting minutes and other legal documents

Ownership of an Indigenous business refers to "beneficial ownership," for example, who is the real owner of the business. Canada may consider a variety of factors to satisfy whether Indigenous persons have true and effective control of an Indigenous business.

##### Documentation for subcontracting

Evidence to support the portion of work done by subcontractors may involve contracts between the contractor and subcontractor, invoices, and payment records. The same evidence requirements apply to subcontractors as to the prime contractors to confirm they are an Indigenous business.

##### Defining Indigenous eligibility for the purposes of set-aside program for Indigenous business

Note : The word “Indian” is used within this section due to its legal context, relating to its significance in the Indian Act .

For the purposes of PSIB , an Indigenous person is defined an Indian, Métis person, or an Inuk who is ordinarily resident in Canada. Evidence of being an Indigenous person includes proof such as:

- Indian registration in Canada
- membership in an affiliate of the Metis National Council or the Congress of Indigenous Peoples or other recognized Indigenous organizations in Canada
- acceptance as an Indigenous person by an established Indigenous community in Canada
- enrollment or entitlement to be enrolled pursuant to a Comprehensive Land Claim Agreement
- membership or entitlement to membership in a group with an accepted comprehensive claim
- evidence of residency in Canada, for example, a provincial or territorial driver's license, lease or other appropriate document

##### Determining ownership and control in Indigenous businesses

For the set-aside program for Indigenous business, assessing whether Indigenous persons have at least 51% ownership and control involves considering various factors:

- capital stock and equity accounts, including preferred stock, convertible securities, common stock classes, warrants, and options
- dividend policies and payments
- existence of stock options to employees
- different treatment of equity transactions for corporations, partnerships, joint ventures, community organizations, cooperatives, etc.
- examination of charter documents like corporate charters, partnership agreements and financial structures
- concentration of ownership or managerial control in partners, stockholders, officers trustees and directors-based definition of duties
- principal occupations and employers of officers and directors to identify their representative interests, such as banker, vested ownerships
- minutes of directors and shareholders' meetings that detail significant operational and directional decisions
- compensation records for executives and employees to gauge the level of effort and responsibility associated with their positions
- nature of the business in relation to the type of contract being negotiated
- cash management practices, such as dividend payments and the status of dividends in arrears
- tax returns to identify ownership and business history
- goodwill contribution/contributed asset valuation to examine and ascertain the fair market value of non-cash capital contributions
- contracts with owners, officers and employees for fairness and reasonableness
- stockholder decisions regarding the appointment of officers, directors and auditors
- trust agreements influencing ownership and control decisions
- partnership terms relating to the distribution of net income, including salaries, capital interest and shared distribution ratios
- litigation records related to ownership disputes
- transfer pricing practices involving non-Indigenous joint ventures
- payments for management or administrative services
- guarantees provided by the Indigenous business collateral agreements

See Treasury Board Contracting Policy Notice 1996-6 Annex A .

#### Decision to set aside

When determining whether to set aside a procurement under PSIB , the decision rests with the client. There are two types of PSIB set-asides, as follows:

##### Mandatory set-asides

A procurement must be set aside under PSIB if the primary recipients or end user of the goods or services are an Indigenous population and the procurement value exceeds $5000. Provided that operational needs, prudence, probity, best value and sound contract management can be assured.

For an Indigenous population to be the primary recipient or end user of the goods or services, delivery does not have to be directly to the Indigenous community. For instance, goods may be delivered to a government site before distribution to Indigenous communities, groups or individuals.

Refer to Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat (TBS) Contracting Policy Notice 1996-2 for information on the meaning of Indigenous population. For examples and more information on mandatory set-asides under PSIB , please contact Indigenous Services Canada .

##### Voluntary set-asides

Clients may choose to restrict any procurement exclusively to qualified Indigenous businesses. You should assist clients in meeting their objectives by highlighting potential opportunities for voluntary PSIB set-asides when qualified Indigenous businesses are known to exist in the marketplace.

If no Indigenous business submits a responsive offer, or if no contract is awarded despite receiving offers to avoid conflicting with sound contracting principles, the solicitation of offers must be reissued. It can be done as either a set-aside or open to all offerors, depending on trade agreement procedures and applicable thresholds.

PSPC will not unilaterally declare a procurement set-aside under PSIB . If a requisition exceeding $5000 identifies an Indigenous population as the primary recipient or end user and is not designated as a PSIB set-aside, you should consult with the client. If the client decides against a set-aside, this decision should be recorded in the file, and the procurement can proceed.

#### Indigenous business certification

Offerors must certify that they are an Indigenous business as defined under the PSIB . This certification must assert the offeror's ongoing compliance with the criteria which define it as an Indigenous business throughout the contract's duration. An offeror’s certification that it is Indigenous is subject to audit, before and after a contract is awarded, to ensure compliance.

#### Subcontracting to Indigenous businesses

Clients may specify that a proportion of subcontracts in open procurements be reserved for Indigenous businesses. This can also include encouraging non-Indigenous offerors to subcontract to Indigenous businesses, through the use of incentives such as additional evaluation points. The inclusion of Indigenous businesses as subcontractors must be clearly identified in the solicitation of offers as an evaluation criterion. Such practices are permitted by all of Canada’s free trade agreements.

#### Set-asides and Canadian content

If the value of a procurement is $25,000 or more, both the PSIB and the Canadian Content Policy will apply simultaneously. Here’s how these guidelines interact within the framework of set-aside procurements:

##### Certification levels

There are two levels of certification under a set-aside procurement. A business must certify its status as an Indigenous business to qualify for consideration. This foundational step ensures only Indigenous businesses are eligible for consideration in the set-aside procurement process.

##### Applying the Canadian Content Policy

After establishing that the procurement qualifies as a PSIB set-aside, the Canadian Content Policy is applied in the same manner as any other procurement. You must determine whether there are sufficient eligible Indigenous businesses to carry out the procurement under one of three categories:

- Solely limited: two or more Indigenous businesses can supply the required Canadian goods or services
- Conditionally limited: potential exists for two or more Indigenous businesses offering Canadian goods or services
- Open: there is an insufficient number of Indigenous businesses able to provide Canadian goods or services; thus, the procurement is open to all qualified Indigenous businesses, regardless of the goods or services’ origin

##### Review process

An offer that includes Canadian content provisions under a set-aside procurement must first be reviewed to ensure the business has provided the necessary certification that it is an Indigenous business. Those offers that meet this criterion are then further evaluated against the Canadian content criteria.

#### Adherence to sound contracting principles

Fundamental to all PSIB procurements is the need to adhere to sound contracting principles. You must always be cognizant of the principles of best value, prudence, probity and operational requirements, in planning your procurement strategy for PSIB set-aside requirements.

#### Red River Métis Business Directory

The Government of Canada recognizes the Manitoba Métis Federation's Red River Métis Business Directory. Businesses listed in the Red River Métis Business Directory are eligible for federal procurement opportunities, such as under the Procurement Strategy for Indigenous Business (PSIB).

### Comprehensive Land Claims Agreements

If you or your clients are aware that a procurement will occur in a location subject to an ongoing Land Claim, or an area for which a Comprehensive Land Claims Agreement (CLCA) or Modern Treaty is being negotiated, you or your client should contact the Indigenous Procurement Policy Case Management Team .

#### Applicability of CLCA s contracting obligations

When procuring goods, services or construction that will be delivered to locations covered by CLCA s, the contracting obligations of each applicable CLCA s apply to each associated portion of the procurement. The final delivery points, which are not necessarily the destination addresses detailed in the requisition, determine the applicability of a CLCA s, not the origin of the requisition.

Additional scenarios where CLCA s procurement obligations may apply include the following:

- procurements in support of government activities within a CLCA s area
- procurements involving performance of services or associated travel by the resulting contractor within a CLCA s area

You should consult with the appropriate advisory body to determine if CLCA s apply in these situations.

The key procedural elements are as follows:

- dollar thresholds: CLCA s apply to applicable procurements of any value
- overlapping areas: in cases where settlement areas of different CLCA s overlap, the obligations of both CLCA s apply. For example, Inuvik, Northwest Territories is situated within the settlement areas of both the Inuvialuit Final Agreement and the Gwich'in CLCA , and so the contracting obligations of both CLCA s will apply to the portion of the procurement with deliveries to Inuvik

- urgent requirements: procurements for urgent requirements must be handled on a case-by-case basis in accordance with the applicable CLCA For procurements that are for pressing emergencies as defined by Treasury Board Secretariat Contracting Policy Notice 2007-4 on Non-Competitive Contracting , you should seek assistance.

#### CLCA evaluation criteria

Several CLCA s contain provisions requiring the inclusion of socioeconomic evaluation criteria in solicitation of offer documents when feasible and aligned with sound procurement management. Such measures are not prohibited by Canada’s international trade agreements. The purpose of socioeconomic criteria is to enhance opportunities for CLCA beneficiaries to experience benefits through:

- subcontracting to land claim beneficiary businesses
- providing employment opportunities for land claim beneficiaries
- offering training and skills development for land claim beneficiaries
- establishing offeror offices within the CLCA regions

Applicable CLCA s include :

- James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement - Inuit Portion
- Inuvialuit Final Agreement
- Nunavut Land Claims Agreement
- Sahtu Dene and Métis Comprehensive Land Claims Agreement
- Tlicho Land Claims Agreement
- Nunavik Inuit Land Claims Agreement
- Labrador Inuit Land Claims Agreement
- Eeyou Marine Region Land Claims Agreement

In addition, solicitations subject to the Gwich'in Comprehensive Land Claims Agreement should include socioeconomic evaluation criteria for the Gwich'in, whenever it is practical and consistent with sound procurement management. Canada's international trade agreements pose no impediment to such measures.

These evaluation criteria can be integrated into the assessment along with price, best value, delivery etc. Offerors should provide proof of their efforts and commitments, which should include the names of persons or companies contacted and details of the engagements at the time of offer submission, as applicable.

If you determine that including CLCA criteria is not practical or consistent with sound procurement management, this decision must be well-documented in the procurement file, preferably in the contract planning and advance approval or procurement plan.

CLCA evaluation criteria should be considered during sole-source negotiations to maximize the socioeconomic opportunities for CLCA beneficiaries.

#### Right of first refusal

In procurements involving locations governed by CLCA s, competition may be restricted to businesses of the applicable CLCA . Specific cases of right of first refusal include the following:

#### James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement

For the Inuit portion of this agreement, Canada prioritizes soliciting offers from businesses within the designated territory. Specifically north of the 55th parallel in Quebec, wherever practicable and consistent with sound procurement management, as outlined in the Agreement Respecting the Implementation of the JBNQA .

#### Provision of commodities

Certain agreements contain a “right of first refusal” for commodities and business ventures. Particularly those contracted out with respect to Parks and the right of first refusal to any new licenses to carry on economic activities involving wildlife and tourism.

#### Priority sourcing

Agreements may specify that CLCA beneficiaries receive “first consideration or first priority” in procurements for services such as silviculture, heritage site management, and technical and support services for surveying the land claims settlement areas.

#### Special considerations

Buyers handling CLCA procurements related to archaeology, forestry, heritage, parks and/or surveying should seek assistance.

Procurements sourced through CORCAN, a special operating agency within Correctional Services Canada, as stores transfer orders are not subject to CLCA s. The following are examples of CLCA applicability:

- generators delivered to Yellowknife, Northwest Territories
- food delivered to a non- CLCA area, then forwarded by the client to a CLCA area
- snowmobiles delivered to a non- CLCA area, where decals are added by the client before shipping to Kuujjuaq, Quebec
- an on-line map selection solution for acquiring mineral claims on Crown lands in Nunavut, accessible to interested companies via internet, with the technical infrastructure and hosting environment located outside a CLCA area
- an aeromagnetic survey to be performed on a CLCA area, with the only tangible deliverable being a final report to be sent to the client in a non- CLCA area

### International trade agreements

International trade agreements incorporate mechanisms that permit Canada to include measures, including but not limited to set-asides, for the benefit of Indigenous Peoples and/or businesses. This includes procurement obligations pursuant to modern treaties (Comprehensive Land Claims Agreements).

Learn more about trade agreements applicability .

When using or relying upon set-asides or exceptions it's important to note that some or all aspects of the procurement may not be subject to certain obligations of trade agreements. Despite these exceptions, you must adhere as closely as possible to the obligations of the trade agreements. To reduce risks, deviations should be made only when necessary and must be justified by the specific purposes of the set-aside or exception.

#### Impact of trade agreements on Indigenous procurements

Canada's free trade agreements pose no impediment to the inclusion of measures for the benefit of Indigenous Peoples and/or businesses in a procurement. When a procurement is set-aside for Indigenous businesses under PSIB , it is no longer subject to the obligations of the trade agreements and does not fall under the jurisdiction of the Canadian International Trade Tribunal.

#### Canadian Free Trade Agreement

Under Article 800: Aboriginal Peoples of the Canadian Free Trade Agreement (CFTA), the CFTA does not apply to any measure adopted or maintained with respect to Indigenous Peoples. If a measure is adopted or maintained with respect to Indigenous Peoples and or businesses in a CFTA -covered procurement, some or all aspects of the procurement may not be subject to CFTA obligations. To reduce risk, you should continue to follow the CFTA obligations to the greatest extent possible. Deviations should be limited and only occur when necessary to implement the measure(s) for Indigenous peoples/businesses.

### Procurement strategy for Indigenous business set-aside checklist

The following set-aside checklist is provided to guide you.

Set-aside determination

Verify if the client has indicated the procurement is set aside for Indigenous businesses.

If no, and the procurement is destined primarily for Indigenous populations and exceeds $5,000, you should inform the client of the potential oversight.

If no, and if qualified Indigenous suppliers exist, you should suggest the possibility of a voluntary procurement strategy for a PSIB set-aside.

If no, and if neither conditions above apply, or the client confirms the procurement is not to be set-aside under PSIB , proceed as per standard procurement policies.

If yes, see below.

#### Comprehensive Land Claims Agreements (CLCA)

Assess if the procurement is subject to CLCA s and determine the extent to which CLCA s and the PSIB set-aside conflict. CLCA s take precedence (see Procurement Strategy for Indigenous Business ).

#### Subcontracting to Indigenous businesses

Confirm if there is a client requirement for subcontracting to Indigenous businesses.

#### Notification

Notify the Business Development Directorate, Indigenous Services Canada (ISC) , upon receipt of a set-aside requirement (see Requisition ).

#### Solicitation of offers documentation

Include the applicable documents outlined in "Requirements for the set-aside program for Indigenous business” and appropriate clauses.

#### Sourcing

Source the requirement according to established policies, procedures, and platforms (see Sourcing of requirements under the Procurement Strategy for Indigenous Business (PSIB) set-asides).

#### Notice of proposed procurement - advanced contract award notice

Ensure the notice includes a statement that the procurement is set aside exclusively for Indigenous businesses (refer to the information on using a source list in Determine the sourcing strategy ).

#### Pre-award audit

Check if ISC has advised whether the procurement is subject to a pre-award audit of certifications before the solicitation closes.

#### Certification of eligibility

Verify that all offerors have provided signed certificates of eligibility with their offers (see Verify offer ).

#### Offer evaluation

Evaluate offers according to the evaluation criteria described in the solicitation of offers documents.

#### Notification of results to ISC

Inform ISC of the two "best-assessed" responsive offerors, excluding financial information.

#### Post-audit advice

Obtain advice from ISC regarding the results of the pre-award audit of certificates of eligibility.

#### Contract award

Award the contract based on established evaluation criteria and the result of the pre-award audit.

#### Contract award notification

Advise ISC of the contract award within 15 working days (see Elements to consider at award – Reporting requirements and award notices ).

#### Contract management

Manage the contract, including advising ISC of any changes in the offeror’s status as an Indigenous business or requests to verify continued eligibility.

#### Procurement reporting

Ensure accurate reporting of the set-aside under PSIB (see Elements to consider at award – Reporting requirements and award notices ).
