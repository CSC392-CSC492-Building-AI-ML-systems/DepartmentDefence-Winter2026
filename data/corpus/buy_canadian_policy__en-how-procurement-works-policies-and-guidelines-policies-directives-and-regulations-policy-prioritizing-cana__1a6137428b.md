# Policy on Prioritizing Canadian Suppliers and Canadian Content in Strategic Federal Procurements

1. 1. Effective date
1. The Policy on Prioritizing Canadian Suppliers and Canadian Content in Strategic Federal Procurements (hereafter, “the Policy”), issued pursuant to the Buy Canadian Procurement Policy Framework, takes effect on December 16, 2025.
2. The Policy does not apply to solicitations published or contracts awarded prior to the effective date.
3. For existing Supply Arrangements (SAs), including any solicitations issued from an existing SA , the Policy applies at the time of the next renewal. Departments may choose to incorporate Canadian suppliers and Canadian content requirements earlier, provided these elements are reflected in the updated Request for Supply Arrangement (RFSA). For existing Standing Offers (SOs), the Policy applies at their next renewal.
2. 2. Purpose
1. The Policy advances the Government of Canada’s mission to build a strong and diversified Canadian economy. It encourages the participation of Canadian suppliers and incentivizes the use of Canadian content in strategic procurement.
2. The Policy utilizes federal purchasing to advance Canada's economic, industrial, and innovation priorities. This includes strengthening domestic supply chains, supporting Canadian businesses, and enhancing national resilience.
3. The Policy encourages the participation of Canadian suppliers by giving them a price-based evaluation credit during the offer evaluation process.
4. The Policy incentivizes the use of Canadian content by evaluating offers based on the inclusion of Canadian goods and/or Canadian services and/or Canadian value-added, as defined in the Policy.
3. 3. Definitions
1. Strategic Procurement means:
- procurement above the financial threshold set out in section 4.4 and that falls within one of the sectors and commodities listed in Appendix A . These sectors are prioritized because they contribute to risk mitigation, drive innovation, strengthen national security and reinforce Canada’s strong and resilient economy.
2. Canadian Supplier means:
- a supplier that has a place of business in Canada where it conducts activities on a permanent basis that is clearly identified by name and accessible during normal business hours; or, a joint venture where each member of the joint venture has a place of business in Canada where it conducts activities on a permanent basis that is clearly identified by name and accessible during normal business hours; and
- is registered and files taxes in Canada (e.g., GST / HST , corporate income tax);
- maintains a registered address in Canada and employs personnel and/or conducts day-to-day business activities in Canada; and
- will not subcontract work to non-Canadian suppliers or individuals located outside Canada, in a manner that results in minimal value-added activities being performed within Canada.
- In assessing whether a supplier is Canadian, the contracting authority:
- may consider the nature of the industry or sector, the geographical location of the supplier, and the level of activity typically performed by other suppliers competing in the same sector;
- must provide the supplier with an opportunity to submit relevant evidence; and,
- must clearly document the rationale and supporting evidence for their decision.
3. Canadian Good means:
- a good wholly manufactured or originating in Canada; or, a product containing imported components that has undergone sufficient change in Canada in a manner that satisfies the definition specified under the Canada-United-States-Mexico Agreement (CUSMA) Rules of Origin. For the purposes of this definition, the reference to "territory of the Parties" in the CUSMA Rules of Origin is to be replaced with "Canada".
4. Canadian Service means:
- a service wholly provided by (natural persons/individuals) based in Canada.
- services include construction services.
5. Canadian Value-Added means:
- in relation to services, a Canadian Service or the proportion of the service contract performed by natural persons based in Canada; and
- in relation to goods, a Canadian Good or the value of the portion of the good produced in Canada or the difference between the dutiable value of the imported goods and the selling price, taking into account any value added by manufacturers and distributors, and including any costs incurred in Canada related to:
- research and development,
- sales and marketing,
- communications and manuals,
- customization, modifications, installation, and support,
- warehousing, distribution, and logistics,
- training and after-sales service.
6. Canadian Content means Canadian Value-Added
7. Applicable trading partner means:
- a country or customs territory listed in the schedule to the Canadian International Trade Tribunal Procurement Inquiry Regulations as a party to one or more of the trade agreements that applies to the procurement process.
8. Supplier of an applicable trading partner means:
- a supplier that has a place of business in an Applicable trading partner where it conducts activities on a permanent basis that is clearly identified by name and accessible during normal business hours; or,
- a joint venture where at least one member of the joint venture has a place of business in an Applicable trading partner where it conducts activities on a permanent basis that is clearly identified by name and accessible during normal business hours, and all other members of the joint venture either meet this requirement or are a Canadian supplier.
4. 4. Application
1. The Policy is issued pursuant to the Buy Canadian Procurement Policy Framework .
2. The Policy supersedes the Interim Policy on Reciprocal Procurement in cases where both apply.
3. The Policy supersedes the Canadian Content Policy in cases where both apply.
4. Financial thresholds are introduced in a phased approach and reflect the total estimated value of the procurement inclusive of all applicable taxes. The Policy applies to all competitive Strategic Procurement where the financial threshold is met:
1. Effective December 16, 2025 : all Strategic Procurement $25M and up published after that date; and
2. Effective June 15, 2026 : all Strategic Procurement $5M and up published after that date.
5. Canada reserves the right to apply the requirements of the Policy to procurements that are otherwise excluded from application, at its sole discretion. Departments and agencies are strongly encouraged to apply the requirements of the Policy to excluded procurements wherever feasible, even below applicable thresholds, to support domestic industry.
6. On a case-by-case basis where strictly necessary, departments and agencies may exclude procurements otherwise subject to the Policy where one or more of the following exceptions applies:
1. Best value – where application of the Policy is expected to result in a cost increase of 25% or more above current market rates, such pricing may be deemed unreasonable or inconsistent with sound stewardship, and may not represent best value for Canada;
2. Public Interest – where the application of the Policy would otherwise be inconsistent with the public interest;
3. No capacity or availability – where the Canadian good is not produced in Canada in sufficient and reasonably available quantities and/or of a satisfactory quality;
4. Undue Delay – where the application of the Policy would result in delays to the procurement of critical defence or national security equipment, which could negatively impact Canadian defence or national security operations;
5. Commercial Off-The-Shelf (COTS) and Military Off-The-Shelf (MOTS) Products.
7. Use of the exceptions listed at section 4.6 must be approved by the minister, of the department or agency for which the procurement is being undertaken.
1. Where the procurement is conducted by a common service provider (e.g., Public Services and Procurement Canada, Shared Services Canada), the minister responsible for the common service provider must also approve the invocation of any exception prior to solicitation. This requirement applies regardless of whether the exception is initiated by the client department.
2. For procurements that are issued by a common service provider on behalf of multiple departments or as part of a government-wide procurement instrument (e.g., National Master Standing Offers), the minister responsible for the common service provider may approve the use of the exceptions under section 4.6 without requiring approval from individual client departments. In such cases, the common service provider must ensure that the rationale and supporting documentation reflect the scope and impact of the exception across all affected departments.
5. 5. Policy Requirements
1. Departments and agencies of the Government of Canada are to implement the requirements of the Policy in their procurement processes. To do so, contracting authorities in collaboration with the business owners are required to:
1. Determine if the Policy applies to the procurement in question, pursuant to sections 4.4 to 4.7; and,
2. Prior to issuing the tender documents, the contracting authority must, if the procurement is subject to the Policy, incorporate the defined terms and associated Canadian Content requirements, outlined in the Policy.
2. Canadian Supplier
3. This requirement seeks to support Canadian industries and ensure that Canadian suppliers are prioritized in procurement processes.
1. When the Policy applies and no exception is applicable, competition is open to Canadian suppliers and, if any international trade agreements apply to the solicitation, Suppliers of an applicable trading partner;
2. Offers from eligible Canadian suppliers, as defined in section 3.2, will receive a 10 percent reduction to the total value of their financial proposal only for the purpose of the evaluation and it will not impact the actual offer price (see Appendix D ).
3. Supplier Eligibility Attestation
4. In order to be considered for the purposes of section 5.2.1, offerors must complete an attestation confirming that they meet the requirements of either definition as part of their offer as outlined in Appendix B .
4. Canadian Content
5. This requirement seeks to support Canadian industries and ensure that Canadian content is prioritized in the procurement process.
1. Selection Methodologies
2. When establishing the requirement of the solicitation, the contracting authority must determine which of the two following methodologies shall be applied for offer evaluation:
1. Point-Rated Methodology
2. Allocate 25 percent of the total evaluation score to the Canadian Value-Added requirement criterion (see Appendix E ).
3. Mandatory Canadian Materials (as defined under the Policy on Prioritizing Canadian Materials) must be excluded from this calculation.
4. Price-Based Methodology
5. Where point-rated criteria methodology may not be suitable, use a price-based evaluation credit 25 percent applied to financial proposal at evaluation. This is only for the purpose of the evaluation and will not impact the actual offer price (see Appendix F ).
6. Mandatory Canadian Materials (as defined under the Policy on Prioritizing Canadian Materials in Federal Procurements) must be excluded from this calculation.
3. Mandatory Minimum
4. The contracting authority in collaboration with the business owner can set a minimum mandatory Canadian Value-Added requirement as part of the evaluation criteria only where justified by procurement objectives and approved as per section 5.7.
5. Applicable Canadian Content Attestation
6. When applicable, offerors must complete an attestation confirming their commitment to use Canadian goods and/or Canadian services and/or Canadian value-added in the performance of the contract requirements as outlined in Appendix C .
6. Contracting authorities must evaluate all offers in accordance with the requirements set forth in sections 5.2 and 5.3.
7. If during the performance of the contract, due to changes in market availability or other substantiated factors, the contractor determines that it is no longer feasible to meet its Canadian content requirement, they may submit a written request to the contracting authority. The request must include detailed justification and supporting documentation.
1. Approval of any such request is subject to the authorization of the client department’s minister, and, where the procurement is conducted by a common service provider (e.g., Public Services and Procurement Canada, Shared Services Canada), the minister responsible for the common service provider, as stipulated in section 4.7 of this Policy.
2. Departments must ensure that contractors continue to meet all contractual obligations including delivery, quality, and performance requirements, while approval is pending. Approval delays do not relieve the contractor of these obligations. If the request is approved, departments must honour valid claims for payment in accordance with the amended terms.
3. The contracting authority may determine that a price adjustment is warranted to offset the difference in Canadian content value. Any such adjustment must be reasonable and informed by sufficient documentation. In the absence of sufficient documentation provided by the contractor, the decision to adjust pricing or contract terms will be at the sole discretion of the contracting authority.
8. Ensure that accurate and complete procurement records are created and maintained in the procurement file.
9. Conditions for Revised Evaluation Criteria, and Selection Methodologies
10. Any changes or revisions to requirements as set forth in sections 5.2 and 5.3 are subject to approvals at the Assistant Deputy Minister level. This includes any adjustments to the reduction to the total value of the financial proposal for Canadian Supplier and modifications to point-rated or price-based methodologies for Canadian Content.
11. Documented Exceptions
12. Approved exceptions must be fully documented by the contracting authority in the procurement file, including the rationale, supporting evidence, and required approvals.
6. 6. Roles and Responsibilities
1. Public Services and Procurement Canada ( PSPC ) is responsible for:
1. Developing and issuing supporting policy instruments, guidance materials, and tools to ensure consistent interpretation and application of the various Canadian priority measures.
2. Common Service Providers ( PSPC and Shared Services Canada) are responsible for:
1. For procurements managed as a common service provider, implementing mechanisms to monitor compliance, tracking performance, and assessing the effectiveness of Canadian priority requirements of procurements under its responsibility.
3. Departments and Agencies are responsible for:
1. Determining applicability of the Policy to each procurement;
2. Ensuring Statements of Work and specifications support Canadian suppliers and Canadian content; and
3. Obtaining and validating (when required) offeror declarations and attestations.
7. 7. Monitoring and Reporting
1. Departments and agencies must:
1. Monitor contractor compliance throughout the procurement lifecycle;
2. Ensure that accurate and complete procurement records are created and maintained to support verification of compliance, including offeror declarations and attestations, and any exceptions where applicable;
3. Support any government-wide data reporting obligations under the Buy Canadian Procurement Framework; and
4. Maintain records of offeror declarations and attestations, and any deviations from contractual obligations.

## Appendix A: Strategic Procurement – List of Sectors and Commodities

### 1. Defence and Security

Goods and services that support Canada’s national defence, military readiness, and security operations, including systems and capabilities essential for sovereignty and protection.

Examples include, but are not limited to: weapons, vehicles, aircraft, ships, naval systems, electronic warfare, logistics, training, security services

### 2. Health and Pharmaceutical

Goods and services related to healthcare delivery, medical treatment, and to ensure public health resilience and emergency preparedness and response.

Examples include, but are not limited to: medical and surgical equipment, healthcare services, nursing care, medical waste, drugs, vaccines and biologicals

### 3. Infrastructure, Construction, and Transportation

Physical assets and systems essential for public and economic activity; services and materials involved in constructing, renovating, or demolishing buildings and structures; and goods and services enabling the movement of people and property.

Examples include, but are not limited to: buildings, transportation infrastructure, mobility, utilities, environmental cleanup, facilities management, aerospace, shipbuilding, motor vehicles

### 4. Information and Communications Technology (ICT)

Digital systems, hardware, software, network infrastructure, and related services that enable government functions.

Examples include, but are not limited to: digital infrastructure, data centers, cloud, networks, telecommunications, 5G, connectivity, software and hardware

### 5. Consumer and Industrial Goods and Materials

Commercial products and materials intended for personal use and consumption or industrial production and operations.

Examples include, but are not limited to: clothing, textiles, furniture, fuel, chemicals and plastics, scientific and testing equipment, fire suppression, machinery.

Professional services directly supporting the above-listed sectors and commodities are also included.

- research and development,

## Appendix D: Canadian Supplier Priority – Financial Evaluation

When compliant offers are received from Canadian suppliers, a 10 percent reduction to the total value of each Canadian Supplier’s financial proposal will be applied.

The following formula will be used:

### FORMULA:

The Offer Price is the total value of the financial proposal submitted by the Canadian Supplier.

0.10 is the 10 percent notional evaluation reduction factor.

The Evaluated Price will be used for evaluation purposes only (does not affect actual offer price).

### EXAMPLE

An offeror submitted an offer with a total value of $100,000 Evaluated Price = $ 100 000 ∙ ( 1 - 0.10 ) Evaluated Price = $ 100 000 ∙ 0.90 = 90 000

For evaluation purposes, the offer price would be considered, for purposes of evaluation, as $90,000 , while the actual offer price remains $100,000 .

## Appendix E: Canadian Value-Added Evaluation Methodology and Scoring

In cases where point-rated criteria methodology is exercised, the following calculation should be used.

Typically, solicitation of offers include, among other things, technical and financial evaluation criteria. In this example, technical evaluation, pricing evaluation and Canadian value-added (CVA) evaluation criteria will be included. The sum of all three weightings (technical, pricing and CVA ) is to equal 100 percent.

The CVA Weighting is 25% of the total evaluation score allocated to CVA . This means that the weightings of the technical and financial portions of the evaluation is 75 points out of 100.

For example, if the percent allotted to the technical merit score is equal 35, the percent for the pricing score would be 40. The weightings in that case would be:

| Column 1 | Column 2 |
| --- | --- |
| CVA evaluation weighting: | 25% |
| Technical evaluation weighting: | 35% |
| Financial evaluation weighting: | 40% |
| TOTAL: | 100% |

### FORMULA:

To calculate a CVA score in an offer, the total dollar value of the Canadian value-added submitted by the offeror is divided by the total dollar value of the submitted offer, then multiplied by the CVA Weighting.

Canadian Value Added Rating = ( $ Value of Canadian Value Added in the offer / $ Value of the offer ) ∙ Canadian Value Added Weighting Note: Mandatory Canadian Materials (as defined under the Policy on Prioritizing Canadian Materials in Federal Procurements) must be excluded from this calculation.

EXAMPLE

A solicitation was launched to acquire goods with a maximum technical merit score of 200 points. The contracting authority used the 25 percent for CVA weighting and allocated 30 percent for technical merit score and 45 percent for pricing score for a total of 100 percent. The solicitation stated that weightings would be allocated as follows:

| Column 1 | Column 2 |
| --- | --- |
| CVA evaluation weighting: | 25% |
| Technical merit weighting: | 30% |
| Pricing weighting: | 45% |
| TOTAL: | 100% |

Three offers were submitted in response to the solicitation:

| | Offer A (Canadian Supplier) | Offer B (Canadian Supplier) | Offer C (Canadian Supplier) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Offer Price | $49M | $47M | $45M |
| Canadian Value-Added (portion of the Offer Price) | $40M | $25M | $30M |
| Pricing Score (45%) | ($45 M/$49 M) * 45 = 41.33 | ($45 M /$47 M) * 45 = 43.09 | ($45 M /$45 M) * 45 = 45 |
| Technical Merit Score (30%) | (175/200) * 30 = 26.25 | (165/200) * 30 = 24.75 | (150/200) * 30 = 22.5 |
| Canadian Value-Added (CVA) Score (25%) | ($40 M/$49 M) * 25 = 20.41 | ($25 M/$47 M) * 25 = 13.30 | ($30 M/$45 M) * 25 = 16.67 |
| Combined Ratings | 87.99 | 81.14 | 84.17 |
| Overall Ranking | 1 | 3 | 2 |

In this example, Offeror A received the highest combined score and would be awarded the contract. Note that the CVA Score that Offeror A received contributed to their offer being the highest overall ranked offer.

## Appendix F: Canadian Value-Added Price-Based Evaluation Methodology and Scoring

Where point-rated criteria methodology may not be suitable, the following formula will be used:

First, determine the Canadian Value-Added Weighting, which is the percentage (X) of the total evaluation score allocated to Canadian Value-Added (25 percent being the default).

Multiply the percentage of Canadian Value-Added by the Canadian Value-Added Weighting . The result will be used as a factor to calculate the adjusted financial score for evaluation. The adjusted financial score for evaluation will be used to credit the financial value of the offer accordingly for evaluation purposes only.

### FORMULAS:

Adjusted Financial Score = $ Value of the offer - ( $ Value of the offer ∙ ( Canadian Value Factor / 100 ) )

Canadian Value Added in the Offer represents the total dollar value of all items that meet the definition of Canadian Value-Added under the Policy, as provided by the Offeror in the Canadian Content Attestation form.

Value of the Offer represents the total evaluated price of the offeror’s submission.

Canadian Value-Added Weighting is the weighting factor established for the purpose of the financial evaluation (see Appendix E ).

### EXAMPLE

An offeror submitted an offer with a total value of $50,000, and their offer contained a mix of goods and services of which $30,000 met the definition of Canadian Value-Added. The contracting authority used the default Canadian Value-Added Weighting of 25 points as described in the Policy. Canadian Value Added Factor = ( $30,000 / $50,000 ) x 25 Canadian Value Added Factor = 15

The Canadian Value-Added Factor was then used in the second formula: Adjusted Financial Score for Evaluation = $ 50 000 - ( 50 000 ∙ ( 15 / 100 ) ) Adjusted Financial Score for Evaluation = $ 50 000 - $ 7 500 Adjusted Financial Score for Evaluation = $ 42 500

The offeror is credited 15 percent per the Canadian Value-Added Factor, which means their evaluated financial proposal would be reduced from $50 000 to $42 500. This results in a more advantageous financial offer due to their Canadian Value-Added content.
