Method of payment The method of payment is the way Canada will pay for work performed or goods delivered, as all arrears (preferred), in advance, as a lot delivery or as each item are delivered. This section provides guidelines for determining the method of payment, including special considerations for multi-fiscal year contracts and international purchases. Determining method of payment Choosing the appropriate method of payment allows you to manage finances responsibly, manage risks, and comply with Canada's financial policies. The most appropriate method of payment must be determined based on the particular procurement. Here are some key elements to consider. Risk assessment Evaluate the financial risks to Canada in scenarios like insolvency, or cancellation of, or default on work. Assess the marketability and potential resale value of work-in-progress against the total of progress payments made by Canada to measure the risk. The disparity between the amount of progress payments and the resale value of inventory is a measure of the risk exposure for Canada. Financing cost estimates For advance or progress payments, calculate the associated financing costs by applying the chartered bank prime lending rate to the cumulative net amount to be financed (i.e. the cumulative cash payout by Canada, less the cumulative value of item deliveries) based on realistic estimates of work progress and delivery timelines. Price reduction evaluation Consider how the method of payment could influence the overall contract price. Advance or progress payments, which reduce the contractor's need for external financing, are expected to result in cost savings for Canada. Financial capacity assessment Confirm that the client possesses adequate funds to support the selected method of payment. Types of method of payment Various methods of payment can be applied depending on the contract's specifics and the client's financial situation. The following are examples of methods of payment. Acquisition card The intent of acquisition cards is to provide a convenient and less burdensome method of procuring and paying for goods and services, while ensuring effective financial control. It is government policy to use acquisition cards for departmental procurement and payment of goods and services (within the levels of procurement authority delegated to departments) where it is efficient, economical and operationally feasible to do so. This policy applies to all organizations considered to be departments within the meaning of section 2 of the Financial Administration Act . See Treasury Board (TB) Directive on Payments for more information on payment by acquisition cards. The Office of the Auditor General conducted a government-wide review of acquisition card usage, and the report stressed the need for better controls and better monitoring by departments. "Corporate acquisition cards" are designed to help eliminate paper in the procurement process. These cards allow an employee to charge purchases for which full payment is made by the department to the applicable card provider. There is no fee for the cards and conditional discounts can be obtained in exchange for faster and/or electronic settlement. Most important, the dollar amount of purchases and monthly limits associated with acquisition cards are controlled by the card-using organization. The use of acquisition cards within federal government departments and agencies offers very significant opportunities for savings in the procurement through payment process. Clients can reduce and simplify the procurement process of goods and services using acquisition cards. Local purchase orders and petty cash are eliminated and a single payment is made for multiple purchases. The ease and flexibility of using the cards provide an incentive to purchase only as the need arises rather than buying in bulk. By using an acquisition card to make the purchase and then settling these purchases electronically, departments can realize significant savings. Taken across government, this approach allows for major cost avoidance in the procurement process. #### Financial capacity assessment

Confirm that the client possesses adequate funds to support the selected method of payment.

##### Criteria for advance payments

You may consider advance payments only under extraordinary circumstances. Your decision process and requirements will vary based on the nature of the contract and it’s value.

For contracts for services exceeding $25,000 :

- a form of guarantee from a financially strong third party is necessary to protect any advance payment
- the guarantee usually takes the form of a surety bond from an associated or parent company or a financial institution, or an irrevocable letter of credit from a Canadian bank. It should provide for return to Canada of the unliquidated balance of the advance, plus interest, in the event of work cancellation or other contract termination for Canada’s convenient. Other types of guarantees may be discussed with a cost analyst
- the need for guarantees can be waived only with a strong business case

For contracts for services valued up to $25,000:

- you may waive the requirement for security, if the contractor has a positive industry reputation, and where no significant financial or performance issues have been recorded in past engagements with Canada

For all contracts, except those for advertising, payment may be made in advance of the due date when the contractor offers a cash discount for advance payment and the discount at least offsets the cost to Canada for early payment.

Note: Cash discounts are not to be considered in the evaluation of offers.
